The Riverside Bookshop

Chapter 1 Before the Rain Stopped

At nine in the evening, Xu Yao was about to pull down the shutter when someone knocked three times.

A girl in a blue raincoat stood outside. The coat reached her knees. Both pockets bulged as though she had hidden two restless birds inside.

“Do you still take old books?” she asked.

Xu Yao switched the counter lamp back on. Instead of a book, the girl took out a tin biscuit box. A little ship was painted on its lid; half the sail had worn away.

Inside were twenty-seven borrowing slips.

Xu Yao picked one up. The paper was thin. It carried a book title, a date, a surname, and half a sentence at the bottom: “When the bridge is finished, I’ll…”

“Where are the books?”

“I don’t know.” The girl pointed into the box. “Grandpa says these all have to come back.”

Outside, a bus crossed the river. Its yellow windows trembled in the water. Xu Yao recognized the handwriting. Its owner used to sit by the shop door, wrapping borrowed books in brown paper. When it rained, he would move his chair a little farther under the eaves.

It was her father’s handwriting.

Chapter 2 An Unfinished Sentence

The shop once had a rule: no deposit, just leave an address. Neighbors lived close enough that a borrower might return with two fish before returning the book.

Xu Yao had stopped that practice when she took over. It wasn’t that she distrusted people. Half the old street had been demolished, and the addresses on the slips were becoming places that no longer existed on a map.

The girl sat on a low stool, catching drops from the eaves with the toe of her shoe.

“Grandpa’s legs aren’t good, so he couldn’t come himself. He says he promised the owner he’d finish the last page.”

Xu Yao spread out the twenty-seven slips. There were a few lines on the back of each one, some careful, some hurried, like notes written while waiting for a bus.

“The boat is late today. There’s fog on the river. I’ve reached chapter three, and suddenly being late doesn’t seem so bad.”

“My daughter tied her own hair for the first time. The two sides don’t match. I didn’t tell her.”

“When the bridge is finished, I’ll take her to a film across the river.”

Together, the scraps held almost an entire summer.

As a child, Xu Yao had often heard her father say that borrowing slips must be kept. She’d thought he was afraid the books wouldn’t come back. One year, when the roof leaked, the first thing he rescued was the drawer beneath the counter where he kept those slips.

“What was the last book your grandpa borrowed?”

The girl gave her a title. Xu Yao searched the shelves and finally found it on the bottom row. Its spine faced inward. The brown-paper cover had been rubbed smooth.

When she opened the last page, a blank borrowing slip fell out.

Chapter 3 Come Back Tomorrow

The rain had stopped. In a puddle by the curb, the shop sign flickered on and off.

Xu Yao called the girl’s grandfather. For a moment, the other end was quiet. Then she heard a cup being moved.

“I returned the book years ago,” he said. “I was moving house and forgot to bring the slips. Later, I kept thinking that summer ought to have an ending.”

Looking at the sentence that had stopped halfway through, Xu Yao asked, “Did you take her to the film?”

“We went. She slept through most of it.” He laughed. “When it ended, she asked me why it had been so short.”

After the call, the girl borrowed a pencil from the counter. She bent over the blank slip and wrote slowly, stopping now and then to ask Xu Yao how to spell a word.

She wrote: “The bridge is finished. Today I brought the slips back for Grandpa. Tomorrow he wants to come himself.”

Xu Yao put the paper in the tin and closed the lid over the ship with half a sail. She left the shutter open. Instead, she moved the boxes beside the door to make room for a chair.

As the girl was leaving, she turned around. “What time do you open tomorrow?”

“Nine.”

“In the morning?”

“In the morning,” Xu Yao said. “But if you’re early, you can knock.”

She stood in the doorway and watched the blue raincoat disappear along the river. Another bus crossed the bridge. The one she’d seen earlier hadn’t been the last after all.
